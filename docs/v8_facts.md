# Facts, version 8 (15 November 2026)

Consolidated facts behind the white paper (Italian) and the preprint (English), version 8.
Result numbers come from `docs/results_v8.md`, generated by `tools/report_v8.py` from the runs.
Inputs and their sources: `model/techs_v8.json`. Audit files: `docs/audit/`.

Conventions used throughout:

- Costs are in real 2025 euros.
- System costs are in EUR per MWh of the 356.6 TWh of demand not met by existing hydropower.
- PI (primo impianto): technologies without commercial plants at their first-plant cost.
- OB (obiettivo): the same technologies at the target cost declared by their makers.
- Nuclear capacity includes small modular reactors (SMRs) unless stated.
- Saving = cost of the least-cost system without any nuclear minus cost of the system with nuclear.
- All results use a 3-hour step and the weather years 2016-2019 unless stated.
- "Within 1%": grid points (4 GW grid) whose system cost is within 1% of the minimum.

## 1. Question and answer

- Question: how much nuclear capacity does the least-cost Italian power system with zero direct emissions contain, for a 2040 demand of 400 TWh?
- Context: in 2026 the Italian Parliament gave final approval to a law that delegates to the government the reintroduction of nuclear power (Senate final vote, 24 September 2026). The law authorises no plant and selects no site.
- Context: the levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) ignores when energy is produced, so it does not measure the cost of meeting demand in every hour.
- Context: Sepulveda et al. (2018) find that firm low-carbon resources lower the cost of fully decarbonised power systems by 10-62%.
- Context: Lombardi et al. (2020) describe near-optimal Italian systems based on solar, wind and synthetic fuels, without nuclear.
- Context: Mastrantuono et al. (2026) find that new nuclear lowers Italian system cost, with an exogenous cap on nuclear, so the optimal amount is not identified.
- The answer rests on three settings: one cost rule for every technology; nuclear at the Dukovany contract price plus owner's costs (10,200 EUR/kW net, the bottom of the bracket the European Commission reports); and a system that does not lean on other countries (balanced annual trade, no foreign capacity in the critical weeks).
- At a 5% real cost of capital: 23.2 GW (44.5% of demand) in PI; 15.7 GW (30.5%) in OB.
- At 7%: 15.6 GW (29.9%) in PI; 11.1 GW (21.6%) in OB, all SMRs.
- The answer is a value curve, not a point. The optimum is flat: in PI at 5% every capacity between 16 and 28 GW costs within 1% of the minimum.
- About 15 GW is in or near the optimum in most of the cost cases from the contract cost upward, not all: on the 4 GW grid, 16 GW is within 1% of the minimum in 11 of 20 combinations of the five cost cases from the contract cost upward and four curves (section 7).
- The model describes a steady state, with 2040 demand and all fleets already built. It is not a transition path and has no limit on the build rate; the time needed to reach 15 GW is outside its scope.

## 2. Model

- Linear capacity-expansion programme, solved with HiGHS (Huangfu and Hall, 2018). All runs reported ended with status optimal.
- One node. Zero direct emissions.
- One fleet serves the four weather years 2016-2019, optimised together.
- 3-hour step: 4 years x 365 days x 8 = 11,680 steps.
- Size per run: 315,000 to 390,000 variables and 365,000 to 475,000 constraints (about twice with eight weather years).
- Objective over Y = 4 years: min Y sum_i c_i K_i + sum_t (sum_i v_i g_i,t + p_m m_t - p_x x_t), with c_i annual fixed cost, K_i capacity, v_i variable cost, m_t imports, x_t exports.
- Decision variables: PV (ground-mounted, agrivoltaic, industrial roofs, other roofs; by zone; finite land), onshore and offshore wind (finite blocks), large nuclear, SMRs, batteries, hydrogen (electrolyser, underground storage, turbine), compressed air, 100-hour storage, new pumped hydro, new hydropower, geothermal, bioenergy, concentrated solar power with storage (CSP), interruptible load, new interconnection.
- Smart charging is not a capacity decision: 25% of electric-car charging can shift within a 12-hour window; only its timing is optimised. Its infrastructure cost was not found and is set to zero, so its benefit is an upper bound.
- Wave energy is in the data file but is off in all 110 runs; it is not an active input.
- Existing hydropower (15 GW, 43.4 TWh a year) and existing pumped hydro are given.
- Demand 400 TWh: base 338, heat pumps 15, electric vehicles 47. This is just below the 404-439 TWh of the Terna and Snam 2024 scenario report.
- Base load: Terna load of 2016-2019, each paired with the same weather year. Solar and wind capacity factors: Pan-European Climatic Database (PECD 2021.3).
- Solar and wind output is bounded by the profile, so curtailment is free.
- New ground-mounted and agrivoltaic PV have trackers: yield 1.12 times the fixed-tilt profile.
- Puglia PV projects filed for environmental assessment declare 1,596-1,712 kWh/kWp, against 1,462 kWh/kWp in the model.
- PV degradation 0.5% a year lowers mean output over the life to 0.94 of the first year. Comparison: Fraunhofer assumes 0.25% a year; the observed US median is 0.75% a year.
- Existing plants kept in the model: 37.8 GW (24.2 GW PV, 13.6 GW onshore wind). They pay operating cost only.
- Existing PV yield is scaled by 0.857, from 1,304 to 1,117 equivalent hours, the national fleet in 2025.
- Existing wind runs 1,600 hours (observed in 2025: 1,626 hours).
- Onshore wind supply: 69.6 GW in blocks from 2,400 to 1,200 full-load hours, existing included.
- Nuclear output moves between 0.5 times the available capacity (minimum load) and the available capacity.
- Nuclear availability shape is an assumption: 95% in winter and summer months, 85% in spring and autumn, mean 90%. The 90% is the load factor in the Commission decision on Dukovany (15.4 TWh a year on 1,952 MW).
- There are no ramp constraints.
- Storage levels are cyclic. Hydrogen has separate capacities for electrolyser, store and turbine.
- Hydropower must produce a given energy in each month, with a minimum flow of 37% of the monthly mean and a maximum power of 15 GW. Monthly budgets are the same in every weather year.
- Adequacy: firm capacity at least 1.1 times the peak load of 76.9 GW (peak of the 3-hour data), that is 84.6 GW (our calculation). The requirement is the same at every time step, so it does not change with a coarser step.
- Capacity credits (assumptions): nuclear, geothermal and new hydropower at their availability; existing hydro (15 GW), existing and new pumped hydro, compressed air and 100-hour storage at full power; batteries at power x 4/6.6; hydrogen turbines and bioenergy at 0.95; interruptible load at 1.0; CSP from its profile in the highest load steps; PV, wind and imports at zero.
- The nuclear share is nuclear output over total demand, so it includes nuclear energy exported in the swap.

Grid charge (transmission only):

- Every new PV kW pays 13 EUR/MWh at 1,066 hours; every new onshore wind kW pays 8 EUR/MWh at 1,709 hours. This is about 240 EUR/kW over 40 years.
- Nuclear pays 2 EUR/MWh. Offshore wind pays no per-MWh charge.
- New capacity above a total wind and PV stock of 137 GW, offshore included, pays a second charge of 13.77 EUR/kW-yr, about 236 EUR/kW.
- With 37.8 GW of existing plants, the second charge starts at 99.2 GW of new plants.
- The charges and the 137 GW threshold are assumptions of the authors, not figures of Terna. The 94 GW and 137 GW values are not in the Terna plan.
- The grid charge follows the discount rate like every other capital cost.
- Distribution grids are not costed.
- PI system without nuclear: new PV 168.84 GW, new onshore 46.00 GW, offshore 2.61 GW (total 217.45 GW). 118.28 GW are above the threshold and pay the second charge (4.57 EUR/MWh).
- In that system the charges are 237.8 EUR/kW (PV), 234.6 EUR/kW (onshore) and 236.2 EUR/kW (second charge); the average over all new PV and wind is 362.7 EUR/kW.
- Terna's 2025 development plan: over EUR 23 billion for 65 GW more renewables by 2030 on the 43 GW installed in 2023. This implies about 350 EUR/kW on average for those 65 GW.
- The Terna plan also serves security and demand, and it does not cover the new PV and wind of the 2040 systems (up to about 217 GW in the PI system without nuclear), whose integration cost is not known.
- Whether our grid charge is higher or lower than the real one is not established.

## 3. Cost rule

- Annual fixed cost: c = K_on x phi(r, T) x CRF(r, L) + F.
- phi(r, T) = (1/T) sum_{k=0}^{T-1} (1+r)^(T-k-1/2): interest during construction, uniform spending over build time T.
- CRF(r, n) = r(1+r)^n / ((1+r)^n - 1): capital recovery over technical life L.
- K_on is the overnight cost, development and grid connection included; contingency, subsidies and grants excluded. F is fixed O&M.
- Real cost of capital r: 5% central, 7% alternative, the same for every technology.
- Real 2025 euros, net capacity, realistic output.
- Three inputs are annual prices and do not go through the formula: hydrogen storage, 100-hour storage, interruptible load.

Why every technology is at today's contract or plant price, nuclear included:

- The decision is taken now. Nuclear must be contracted now, at today's prices, because it takes about ten years from decision to operation.
- The alternatives are compared the same way. PV and batteries are built in one or two years. The hydrogen that would replace nuclear in the winter weeks is the last part of the system, built just before fossil plants close.
- Future costs for the alternatives only would compare a contract with a forecast. Future costs for all, nuclear series included, would compare two forecasts.
- Projections for PV, batteries and nuclear built in series all point down, and none is an observed cost. Using them for some technologies and not others would break the one-rule symmetry.
- No 2040 cost projection is used for any technology. The only cost declines are the OB target costs. A 2040-cost variant was withdrawn before running.
- Every technology enters at its contract or plant price without contingency. This applies to nuclear as to the others.
- Every correction applied to one technology was checked on all the others: owner's costs, net capacity, interest during construction, life, operating cost, realistic output.

Auction prices are not costs:

- FER-X (two-way contracts for renewables), first auction, December 2025: 56.82 EUR/MWh for solar. It can be combined with capital grants of up to 40%, in which case the tariff is cut by up to 35%, and with tax credits.
- MACSE (storage capacity procurement), first auction: 12,959 EUR/MWh-yr. This corresponds to about 95 EUR/kWh for batteries of about 6.6 hours. The ARERA reference of 150.5 EUR/kWh is for 4-hour batteries. They are not the same product.
- According to analysts, MACSE bids relied on revenues after the contract and on low returns.
- Auction prices implied capital costs below observed ones: about 681 EUR/kW for ground PV.

Source types:

- Official or regulatory: nuclear contract price, build time and operating cost (Commission decision); batteries and interruptible load (ARERA); roof PV, wind and PV O&M (GSE study for ARERA); electrolysers (IEA).
- University and industry: ground-mounted and agrivoltaic PV and wind (Politecnico di Milano; Italia Solare).
- Press: Dukovany owner's costs; SMR costs.
- Studies, single projects or makers' targets: hydrogen storage, compressed air, 100-hour storage.
- Most costs are of plants contracted or built in 2024-2026. The CSP first-plant cost (Andasol, 2008) and the Barakah cost case (a 2009 contract) are older and revalued.
- 10,200 EUR/kW is our sum of public figures, not an official or notified figure.

## 4. Nuclear cost

Central case: Dukovany 5-6, two APR1000 reactors of Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power (KHNP), Czech Republic.

- Main source: the European Commission decision to open the in-depth investigation of the Czech state aid, case SA.117794, OJ C/2026/2825, published 27 May 2026.
- The two-unit aid package is under in-depth investigation by the Commission. It has not been approved.
- The contract price is in current prices with escalation clauses. Works are planned from 2029; first grid connection December 2036 (unit 5) and December 2037 (unit 6). The figures are not out-turn costs.
- That an Italian plant would cost as much as Dukovany is an assumption.

The central value in seven steps:

- 1. EPC contract signed with KHNP in June 2025: CZK 407 billion. The fuel contract is separate (CZK 15-20 billion for about ten years) and is not inside the CZK 407 billion.
- 2. State preparation and owner's costs: CZK 85 billion (Czech television CT24, 13 October 2025). This figure is not in the decision. It is inside the Commission's bracket of EUR 3.0-5.0 billion for development capital expenditure.
- 3. Exchange rate: 2025 average ECB reference rate, 24.688 CZK per euro. CZK 492 billion = EUR 19.93 billion.
- 4. Net capacity: sellable capacity "up to 976 MWe" per unit (1,055 MWe is gross), 1,952 MW in all, 15.4 TWh a year, a 90% load factor (recitals 40-41).
- 5. Overnight cost: EUR 19.93 billion / 1,952 MW = 10,210 EUR/kW net, rounded to 10,200 (our calculation). The EPC contract alone is 8,446 EUR/kW net.
- 6. Czech financial model (recitals 89 and 95): 9-year construction, 60 years of operation, OPEX 35-45 EUR/MWh (fixed O&M, lifecycle investment, fuel, waste account, decommissioning). Midpoint 40 EUR/MWh used: 27.75 fixed at a 90% load factor and 12.25 variable.
- 7. Annual fixed cost: phi(5%, 9) = 1.255, CRF(5%, 60) = 0.0528 (our calculation). fixN = 10,200 x phi x CRF + 27.75 x 0.9 x 8.76 + grid = 676 + 219 + 16 = 911 EUR/kW-yr. The grid term is the 2 EUR/MWh charge. fixN is 1,239 EUR/kW-yr at 7% (1,239.3 in the runs).
- OPEX at 35 EUR/MWh would lower fixN by about 39 EUR/kW-yr; at 45, raise it by about 39.

Why the central case is the bottom of the Commission bracket:

- The decision (recital 93) gives only a bracket for the overnight cost of both units: EUR 20-25 billion in 2024 prices, with EPC, fuel, owner's costs and contingency.
- Without fuel (EUR 0.709 billion) this is 9,890-12,450 EUR/kW net in 2024 euros and 10,090-12,706 EUR/kW net in 2025 euros.
- 10,200 EUR/kW sits at the bottom of the bracket: a plant built without spending the contingency.
- This is the same rule used for every technology, each at its contract or plant price without contingency. A plan is made to build well, and the decision maker needs to know what nuclear is worth in that case.
- The rest of the bracket is read as cost cases: midpoint 11,400 EUR/kW and top 12,700 EUR/kW (2025 euros).
- For this reason the cost of nuclear is not a limit of the study. Every cost case is read on the value curve.

## 6. Value curves and how to read them

Method:

- C(N; f) is the minimum system cost per MWh with nuclear fixed at N and its annual fixed cost f.
- With N fixed, f multiplies a constant and changes neither the feasible set nor the rest of the optimal choice. So C(N; f') = C(N; f) + (f' - f) N / E, with E = 356.6 TWh a year.
- One set of runs at the central cost gives the cost curve of every cost case that differs only in fixN.
- Marginal value of the block from N1 to N2: v(N1, N2) = f - [C(N2; f) - C(N1; f)] E / (N2 - N1). It does not depend on f: it is a property of the rest of the system.
- C is the optimal value of a linear programme as a function of a right-hand side, hence convex in N, so v falls as N grows.
- A block is worth building when its value exceeds the fixed cost of nuclear. The optimum for a cost case is where the curve crosses that cost.
- C(N) was computed for N = 0, 4, ..., 40 and 48 GW in PI and OB at 5% and 7%, and for N = 4, ..., 36 GW in OB without SMRs (batch 5, 20 runs), whose N = 0 point is the run with no nuclear.
- Runs at fixN 911.0 (5%) and 1,239.3 (7%).
- The fixed N is large reactors. In PI, SMRs at first-plant cost remain available and play no material role.
- OB has two curves. Without SMRs: the value of nuclear as a whole, the OB curve used unless stated. With SMRs available at target cost: shows the substitution between large and small reactors.

## 8. Financing

- Three variables that policy can influence decide nuclear's annual fixed cost: the cost of capital, the build time and the construction cost.
- A real rate near 5% corresponds to public or guaranteed financing.
- Dukovany package (under investigation): a preferential state loan with a two-way contract whose strike price is in a bracket of 85-105 EUR/MWh in 2024 prices, and a target return on equity of 6-8% (recitals 91 and 106).

## 9. Trade rule

Central rule:

- Balanced annual swap: in every weather year imports equal exports (no net imports, no net exports).
- Imports cost 115.94 EUR/MWh, the 2025 average Italian price (PUN). Exports earn 61.07 EUR/MWh, the 2025 average French day-ahead price.
- Import capacity today: 11,535 MW (ARERA 366/2025, six borders). Up to 7.33 GW of new lines can be built at their cost.
- Exports are limited to 6,095/11,535 of the import capacity. Terna limits: north border 10,435 MW import and 4,995 MW export; east border 1,100 MW each way. The 6,095 MW export total is our sum (4,995 + 1,100).
- In the critical weeks imports are not available and interconnection has no capacity credit.
- Critical weeks: the three weeks of each year with the highest mean residual load, defined as load minus the output of a reference mix of 100 GW of PV and 40 GW of onshore wind.
- The neighbouring systems are not simulated.

Why the rule is a planning standard, not a limit or a bias:

- A national plan must work if every neighbour planned the same way. If every country counted on the others' plants in the critical weeks, those plants would not be enough for all.
- The critical weeks all fall in November-February (section 12).
- In 2024, 85% of Italy's imports came from Switzerland (43.1%) and France (41.6%). Net imports in 2024 were 51 TWh, 16.3% of demand.
- In winter France has its own peak, and its firm capacity is nuclear.
- Switzerland was a net importer on almost every day of winter 2025/26 (from mid-October), with the Goesgen nuclear plant offline.
- France was a net importer in 2022, when it had to stop reactors.
- Relying on imports in those weeks means relying largely on neighbours' nuclear plants when they are needed at home. A system without Italian nuclear built that way imports nuclear.
- How much foreign capacity will be free in winter 2040 cannot be known or guaranteed, so the central case does not count on it.
- The rule is the condition for comparing systems that are really different. It is not listed among the limits.
- Today Italy depends on imports: in 2025 it imported 55.2 TWh and exported 7.4 TWh (net 47.8 TWh).
- Trade is still used to move energy in time: the PI system with nuclear swaps 17.7 TWh a year in each direction.

The previous version's trade rule (a variant with its price):

- It is a bundle of five changes: 38% of the import capacity available and counted as firm in the critical weeks; weeks chosen by low PV and wind output; import price x1.17 in those weeks; no exports; no annual balance.
- Under it the model builds all 7.33 GW of new lines. Italy imports 52.0 TWh a year (PI with nuclear), 57.7 (PI without), 56.0 (OB with), 70.7 (OB without), and exports nothing.
- So the previous rule is about today's dependence, and the central case is a choice to end it.
- Price of self-sufficiency (central rule against the previous rule, 5%):

- Self-sufficiency costs more without nuclear because the system without nuclear leans more on its neighbours.
- Ending the dependence roughly doubles optimal nuclear.
- The direction of the previous rule as a limit is uncertain: it depends on free foreign winter capacity in 2040.
- The export price, an annual average of the French price, is the remaining uncertainty in the central rule (section 14).

## 14. Limits with their direction

Not limits:

- The nuclear construction cost at the contract price is not a limit: it follows the same rule as every technology, and the other costs are read on the curve as cost cases.
- Today's prices for every technology are not a limit: the decision is taken now (section 3). Projections all point down; none is an observed cost and none is used.
- The trade rule is a planning standard, not a limit (section 9).
- Financing nuclear at about the alternatives' rate is a condition of the recommendation, not a limit (section 8).

Simplifications that make the result cautious for nuclear:

- 3-hour step, hourly data not rebuilt: 6 to 3 hours adds 1.6 GW (PI) and 1.9 GW (OB); the 1-hour value is not known.
- Four weather years: eight years with the four worst give 27.1 GW in PI; OB about unchanged (15.5 against 15.7).
- One node, and the same hydro budget every year with no dry years: both favour the system with more PV. Not quantified.

Limits that favour nuclear (removing them would lower optimal nuclear capacity):

- Caps on ground-mounted and agrivoltaic PV are our choice; every such block is at its cap in the PI system without nuclear. Not quantified.
- Demand flexibility limited to smart charging (25% of electric-car charging) and to interruptible load. Not quantified.
- No common-mode outages of the nuclear fleet.
- Assumed availability shape, 95% in the peak months.
- Nuclear load factor of 87.5% (PI) and 89.1% (OB) results from the optimisation with 90% mean availability and no ramp constraints. It may be optimistic against fleets that modulate (France 2024: 67%, authors' estimate from the audit file). The world fleet had 83% in 2024; the IAEA median is 86.4%.

Limits that favour the system without nuclear (removing them would raise optimal nuclear capacity):

- Distribution-grid reinforcement not costed; it serves mostly rooftop and distributed PV, larger in the system without nuclear. Not quantified.
- Hydrogen storage at 3.76 EUR/kg, the low part of the 2.06-14.95 EUR/kg range of a desk study; electrolysers at target cost in OB. Not quantified.
- Existing wind kept at operating cost although beyond its life in 2040. Not quantified.
- Perfect foresight over the weather years. Not quantified.
- Neighbours not simulated; export price is an annual average, while a solar-heavy system's surplus comes when neighbours also have a surplus. This favours the system that exports more, the one without nuclear (27.7 against 17.7 TWh a year in PI).

Both directions or unknown:

- Grid charge (about 240 EUR/kW on all new PV and onshore wind plus about 236 above 137 GW in total; average about 363 in the PI system without nuclear, against about 350 implied by the Terna plan). Check: 26.7 GW in PI, 19.1 GW in OB.
- Nuclear OPEX 40 EUR/MWh, midpoint of 35-45: 35 lowers fixN by about 39 EUR/kW-yr, 45 raises it by 39.
- Capacity credits (batteries at power x 4/6.6, hydro at 15 GW in full) and build times of several minor technologies are assumptions. Not quantified.
- CSP ceiling of 10 GW, reached in both OB systems (our assumption). Not quantified.
- Steady state: no limit on the pace of construction and no transition path. The study says how much nuclear to have, not how fast it can be built.

## 15. Recommendation

- Plan a nuclear fleet of about 15 GW: about 15 APR1000 units of 976 MW net, about 30% of 2040 demand.
- It is the part justified in most cases (11 of 20 cells, section 7): the optimum with alternatives at target cost (OB 5%: 15.7 GW), with capital at 7% and first-plant costs (PI 7%: 15.6 GW), and with nuclear at the top of the Commission bracket in PI (14.0 GW).
- At 5%, about 15 GW is in or near the optimum with nuclear between the contract cost and the midpoint of the bracket (midpoint: 16 GW in PI, 12 GW in OB). At 7% it is in or near the optimum in PI at the contract cost.
- Keep the option to grow to about 23 GW if hydrogen, compressed air and CSP do not reach their target costs (PI 5%: 23.2 GW; 16-28 GW within 1% of the minimum cost).

Three conditions:

- 1. Built at the Dukovany contract cost: about 10,200 EUR/kW net including owner's costs, in 9 years.
- 2. Nuclear financed at about the same real rate as the alternatives: 5% real for every technology in the central case. With 7% for every technology, about 15 GW holds only with first-plant costs for the alternatives.
- 3. A system that does not rely on foreign capacity in the critical weeks.

Cases where the recommendation changes:

- Capital at 7% for every technology together with alternatives at target cost: 11 GW, as SMRs.
- Nuclear financed at a higher rate than the alternatives: nuclear at 7%, rest at 5%: 4 GW in PI (0-12 within 1%, saving 0.93) and 0 in OB without SMRs (0-4), and SMRs at 7% would not enter either. Nuclear at 6%: 16 GW in PI (8-20) and 8 GW in OB (4-12).
- Staying with today's import dependence (previous trade rule, which counts on foreign capacity in the critical weeks and on net imports of 52-71 TWh a year): 7-12 GW.

Where 15 GW does not hold (two unfavourable conditions combined):

- OB at 7%: 11.1 GW, all SMRs, whenever large reactors cost at least the contract price. With large reactors only at the contract cost, 16 GW is still within 1% of the minimum (one of the 11 cells).
- OB at the top of the bracket: 8 GW with large reactors only, 0-12 GW within 1%, saving 0.88 EUR/MWh; 14.4 GW as SMRs at target cost.
- 7% with the top of the bracket: 4 GW in PI (0-12 within 1%); no large reactors in OB, where SMRs stay at 11.1 GW.
- 7% with the risk median: 8 GW in PI; no large reactors in OB (SMRs 11.1 GW).
- 7% with the Vogtle cost: no large reactors in PI (0-4 within 1%) or OB (SMRs 11.1 GW).
- OB at the Vogtle cost: no large reactors (0-8 within 1%); about 14.4 GW of SMRs at target cost.
- In PI the first 4 GW are worth building in every cost case examined at 5%, Vogtle included.
- Construction cost and the rate at which nuclear is financed are the first things to verify.
- Because the headline value changed several times, readers should check the curve, the inputs and the error register, which are public.
